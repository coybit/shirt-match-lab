Shirt Match Lab

The childhood card game, rebuilt from real kits. Each card is a footballer, and the card’s colour is the colour covering most of his shirt, measured from Wikipedia’s kit data for about 22,700 teams. Lay a card of the same colour as the last one played and you take the whole pile.

The game

Two players split the deck. Before each set you may reorder your own cards, which you can see but your opponent can’t. During a set the order is locked, and the players take turns laying the top card face up.

Each card has one colour: the colour that covers most of the shirt. If two colours cover about the same area, a preset order decides (red, blue, green and other strong colours before black, grey and white). If the card you lay has the same colour as the card just laid, you win the whole pile. The winner starts the next set. You lose when it’s your turn and you have no cards.

Your card k is only ever compared with the opponent’s cards right before and right after it. That one fact drives most of the strategy.

Which colours go together

Lift = how often two colours share a shirt, compared with chance. Above 1 means teams seek the pair out. Near 0 means it’s avoided. Most pairs score below 1, because a shirt rarely has more than two colours.

A few shirts dominate (Zipf’s law)

Colour combinations ranked by frequency, on log–log axes. A roughly straight line means a power law: the 2nd combination is much rarer than the 1st, the 10th rarer still, like word frequencies in a language.

Where the game meets probability distributions

Poisson: rare colours in your hand

How many cards of one colour land in a 20-card hand? Exactly Binomial(20, p). When the colour is rare, that’s almost identical to Poisson(20p): the “law of rare events”. Common colours break the approximation.

Not Poisson: colours per shirt

This looks at every colour on the real shirts, before they’re reduced to one card colour. If colours were added to shirts at random, the count would look like 1 + Poisson. Real shirts are more regular: designers stop at two colours, so three-colour shirts are much rarer than Poisson predicts. Statisticians call this under-dispersion.

Geometric: how long a set lasts

Waiting for the first match is like waiting for the first head when flipping a coin, which gives a geometric distribution. It isn’t exactly geometric, though. The chance of ending at each card falls after the first comparison, because a set that survives has drifted towards rare colours. It settles at the largest eigenvalue of the “no-match” matrix (Perron–Frobenius).

The birthday problem

With 365 birthdays you need 23 people for a 50% chance of a shared birthday. Shirts have only about six common colours, so a shared colour is almost guaranteed within five cards.

Coupon collector

How many cards must you see before every common colour (over 2% of shirts) has appeared? The rarest colour sets the wait. The theory says about n·(1 + 1/2 + … + 1/n) for n equally common colours, and unequal colours take longer.

Random walk: how long a game lasts

Under random play your card count is a fair random walk (gambler’s ruin). Game lengths are very spread out: the standard deviation is about as large as the mean, with a long right tail, typical of first-passage times.

1 · Probability: the match chance

Write each card as the set of its colours, x ⊆ {white, red, …}. Two cards match unless the sets are disjoint. With card types appearing with probability p(x):

P(match) = Σx Σy p(x) p(y) · [x ∩ y ≠ ∅] d(x) = P(random card misses x) = Σ_{y ⊆ complement(x)} p(y)

d(x) is a subset sum over the Boolean lattice. All 2F values come from one zeta transform, the inverse of Möbius inversion on that lattice.

2 · Markov chain: second-mover advantage

Under random play a set is a Markov chain whose state is the last card laid. The set ends at card L with probability below. Even L means the second player won.

3 · Absorbing chain: who wins the game

Over a whole game the state is (A’s cards, who starts). Solving (I − Q)·w = b with the fundamental matrix gives exact win odds and expected length under random play. The curve is almost the straight line k/N: random play is a fair game (martingale), like gambler’s ruin.

4 · Graph: the colour network

Each colour is a node sized by how often it appears. Each edge is a colour pair worn on the same shirt, with thickness showing how often. A card is a clique in this graph, and two cards match exactly when their cliques share a node.

5 · Matrix: compatibility & eigenvector

Kij = 1 if shirt types i and j share a colour. Then P(match) = pᵀ K p is a quadratic form. The Perron eigenvector of K ranks shirts by how “central” they are: how many other shirts they hit, weighted by how central those are in turn.

6 · Algebra: symmetry & permutations

Colour symmetry. The rules don’t care which colour is which. Any relabelling σ ∈ SF of the F colours maps games to games. Strategies that only count colours, such as “colourful first”, are invariant under the whole group. Real kit frequencies break that symmetry, and that’s exactly what the smarter strategies exploit.

Deck orders. A hand of n cards has n! orders, the symmetric group Sn. The optimiser walks it by transpositions (swapping two cards), which generate all of Sn. Only adjacent pairs enter the set-win formula, so the objective is a sum of terms over consecutive cards. That makes it a travelling-salesman-style path problem on a graph whose edge weights are d(a) − d(a ∪ b).

Lattice. Card types live in the Boolean lattice 2F (union = join, intersection = meet). A match is a non-zero meet, or, read as 0/1 vectors, a non-zero dot product. Non-matching cards are orthogonal vectors on the hypercube {0,1}F.

7 · Geometry: carving up colour space

Naming a colour means cutting the RGB cube into regions. Below, the hue × lightness plane is coloured by the region each point falls in. Dots are real kit colours, which pile up on the region borders. That’s why the “min share” setting and the colour scheme change the odds so much.

8 · Sensitivity: how the colour rule changes the odds

Small choices in how a shirt becomes a card colour change the game. In the every-colour variant, the cliff near 8–10% comes from collars and trims: below it, almost every shirt “has” white.

Where the data comes from

  • Kits: English Wikipedia infoboxes for clubs and national teams. Home, away and third kits, as body, sleeve and pattern colours, scraped on 17 Oct 2026.
  • Stripes and hoops: about 16,000 kit pattern images from Wikimedia Commons, read pixel by pixel. Many striped shirts list only their base colour in the text.
  • Teams: Wikidata for country, continent, league and fame (number of Wikipedia language editions).
  • History: 2,012 season articles from 43 well-known clubs, 1880s–2020s.

Assumptions you can change

All of them are set in the Deck tab, and the project defaults live in config/assumptions.json.

  • Kit usage: home 68%, away 24%, third 8% of matches. This is an estimate, because no database records the kit worn in every match. Per-team exact figures can go in data/overrides/kit_usage_overrides.json.
  • Matches per season: clubs 38–48 depending on league level, national teams 10.
  • Fame weight: Wikipedia language editions raised to a power, optionally keeping only the top N teams, as a card pack would.
  • Card colour: the colour covering the largest share of the shirt (body 70%, sleeves 15% each, stripes measured from the kit images). Colours within 5 points of each other count as a tie, decided by the preset order. A variant where every colour covering ≥12% counts is available in the Deck tab.

Known gaps

  • Kits are the current season’s. The history sample covers 43 big clubs only, and national-team history isn’t included yet.
  • League labels come from Wikidata and can name a club’s former league.
  • Sleeve patterns (only the body pattern was analysed) and shorts and socks are ignored, since the game is about shirts.
